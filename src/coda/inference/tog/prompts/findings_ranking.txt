Choose the underlying cause of death for a verbal autopsy case.

A first opinion proposed candidate causes. For each candidate you have the findings typical of it that the case shows. Each finding is a question that the case answers the way its grade describes, tagged with that answer. Its grade comes from ProbBase, the knowledge base of the InterVA model, and is an expert rating, on a rough logarithmic scale, of how often deaths from that cause give that answer. Read it as the conditional probability P(answer | cause), not as how likely the cause is given the answer: 'I' means always (1.0), 'A+' about 0.8, 'A' about 0.5, 'A-' about 0.2. For example, "Did (s)he have a fever? (grade A+ for Malaria on answer yes)" means about 80% of malaria deaths have a fever, and "Did the baby ever cry? (grade A for Birth asphyxia on answer no)" means about half of birth asphyxia deaths are answered no. Typical findings that the case answers the other way, or does not mention, are only counted: as in InterVA, they are not evidence against the cause. A finding graded high for several candidates does not tell them apart.

These findings act as foundational knowledge for each cause of death that is also relevant to the specific verbal autopsy case.

The true cause will likely be among the first opinion's proposed candidates, especially if the narrative is detailed and descriptive; but you may choose another listed cause if the evidence points to it. In your final answer, give calibrated probabilities that sum to about 1.

When citing evidence, copy exactly from what is listed under CANDIDATES, not text from the case: at most {max_cited} findings into cited_findings, without their tag or grade. Cite only those that decided the ranking, and leave the list empty if none did. When giving your reasoning, explain in 1-2 sentences what decided the ranking, including whether and how the findings influenced it.

Before setting any probability, fill candidate_updates. Give an entry for every candidate and for any cause you add. Compare the evidence with the cause's first-opinion probability and choose a direction: 'up' if specific evidence should raise it, 'down' if specific evidence should lower it, and 'no_change' if nothing justifies moving it. In evidence, name what justifies that direction, or say that nothing does. A cause's overall strength is not a reason to move it: a likely cause can go down, and an unlikely cause can go up. Then set the probabilities in top_causes to match these directions. A no_change cause keeps its first-opinion probability.
